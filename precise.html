PRECISE — A Framework for Selecting Quality Indicators

A structured seven-criterion framework for evaluating and selecting robust quality indicators in Acute Medicine QI projects

Choosing the wrong measure is one of the most common reasons AMU improvement projects stall. A measure that cannot detect the change you are making, that is defined ambiguously, or that staff cannot collect without significant extra burden will generate months of frustration and inconclusive data. The SAM-QI Committee developed PRECISE as a practical seven-criterion test that can be applied to any proposed indicator before committing time and resource to data collection — a quality assurance step for your measurement plan, not an afterthought.

Each letter corresponds to a question that every candidate indicator must be able to answer. If it cannot, the indicator needs replacing or refining before the project begins. SAMBA's Clinical Quality Indicators (CQIs) have been developed with many of these principles in mind, making them a strong starting point for any AMU project. The PRECISE scorecard in the SAM QI Toolkit above can be used to evaluate any novel indicator your team proposes — whether you are a nurse designing a pressure injury surveillance measure, a pharmacist proposing a medicines reconciliation indicator, or an AHP developing a functional assessment metric.

P

Patient-centred & Patient-important

Does this indicator matter to patients?

The indicator must reflect something that matters to patients — not just what is convenient to measure. It should be linked to patient experience, outcomes, safety, or wellbeing, and where possible co-designed with patient input.

In Acute Medicine, patient-important indicators include: time to senior clinical review, explanation of diagnosis, discharge information quality, and time to pain management — all captured in SAM-PLER patient experience data.

  • Is this something a patient would recognise as important to their care?
  • Does it appear in patient-reported outcome or experience frameworks?
  • Is there evidence it correlates with patient-perceived quality?
❓ "Would a patient on this AMU agree this is worth measuring?"
R

Reliable & Reproducible

Can different observers measure this consistently?

A reliable indicator produces the same result when measured by different observers at the same time (inter-rater reliability) or by the same observer at different times (intra-rater reliability). An unreliable indicator generates noise rather than signal.

Common reliability threats in AMU QI include: subjective clinical judgements, inconsistent data entry, free-text documentation (as opposed to structured fields), and variation in NEWS2 recording practices.

  • Is there a clear, unambiguous operational definition?
  • Could two different data collectors reach the same value independently?
  • Is it drawn from structured data fields (e.g., EPR) rather than free text?
❓ "Would two data collectors independently arrive at the same number?"
E

Evidence-based

Is there evidence linking this indicator to quality?

There must be published evidence — from clinical guidelines, national audits, or peer-reviewed literature — that the indicator is linked to quality of care or patient outcomes. An indicator without an evidence base measures activity, not quality.

In Acute Medicine, the evidence base is strongest for: NEWS2 scoring and escalation (NICE NG94), Sepsis 6 bundle components (UK Sepsis Trust / Surviving Sepsis Campaign), time to specialist review (RCP standards), and SDEC utilisation (NHS England SDEC operational standards).

  • Is it drawn from a clinical guideline, national standard, or peer-reviewed study?
  • Is there evidence that higher performance on this indicator leads to better outcomes?
  • Is it endorsed by SAM, RCP, NHS England, NICE, or another authoritative body?
❓ "Where is the evidence that measuring and improving this leads to better patient care?"
C

Clear & Comparable

Is it unambiguously defined and nationally benchmarkable?

A clear indicator has a precise, unambiguous operational definition — including numerator, denominator, data source, time window, and exclusion criteria. Comparability means the indicator can be used to benchmark against other units, including SAMBA national data.

Lack of clarity is one of the most common reasons QI indicators fail. "Sepsis recognition" is not clear; "proportion of patients with suspected sepsis who have NEWS2 ≥5 and received IV antibiotics within 60 minutes of presentation" is clear.

  • Is there a written operational definition (numerator / denominator / time window)?
  • Are exclusion criteria specified?
  • Is the indicator defined in a way that enables comparison with SAMBA or other AMUs?
❓ "Could you hand this definition to a colleague and have them collect identical data?"
I

Improvable & Impactful

Is there genuine room for improvement and does it matter?

An indicator must show meaningful variation — both between the current state and achievable best practice, and ideally between different units nationally (via SAMBA). There is no point measuring something that is already at ceiling performance, or where improvement would have negligible clinical impact.

Use your baseline data and SAMBA CQI benchmarks to assess improvability. If your unit already achieves ≥95% on an indicator and the national average is 96%, there is little room or value in running a QI project targeting that measure.

  • Is there a demonstrable gap between current performance and best practice?
  • Does SAMBA data show variation between units, suggesting improvability?
  • Would closing the gap make a meaningful difference to patient outcomes?
❓ "Is there enough gap between where we are and where we should be to justify a QI project?"
S

Sensitive to Change

Will the indicator detect the improvement if it occurs?

A measure that is insensitive to the specific intervention being tested will fail to demonstrate improvement even when genuine change occurs. Statistical sensitivity is distinct from clinical importance — a measure can be important but insensitive to a particular intervention.

Consider whether the indicator moves quickly enough to be detected within the timeframe of your PDSA cycles. Also consider the denominator: if the event is very rare (e.g., cardiac arrest rate), even large relative improvements may not register statistically in a small AMU dataset.

  • Will this indicator change measurably within the planned project timeframe?
  • Is the denominator large enough to detect change with adequate statistical power?
  • Is the measure proximal (close to the intervention) or distal (far downstream)?
❓ "If our intervention works, will this indicator actually show a detectable signal?"
E

Evaluable & Efficient to Collect

Can the data be collected at reasonable cost and burden?

Even the best-designed indicator is useless if the data cannot be collected reliably, consistently, and without excessive resource. Feasibility of measurement is a core requirement. Consider whether the data is already collected routinely (EPR, SAMBA, PAS), or whether it requires additional data collection that will burden clinical staff.

High data collection burden leads to incomplete datasets, biased sampling, and team burnout — undermining project sustainability. Where possible, align your indicators with existing data flows: SAMBA CQIs, EPR audit reports, coding data, or nursing observation charts.

  • Is the data already collected routinely in EPR, SAMBA, or clinical records?
  • What is the time and resource burden of data collection on the team?
  • Can measurement be sustained beyond the active project phase?
❓ "Can we collect this data consistently without it becoming a burden that undermines the project?"
PRECISE Criteria Mapped to the Donabedian Triad

The Donabedian framework (Structure → Process → Outcome) provides the conceptual backbone for organising quality indicators. All three indicator types should be included in a complete AMU QI measurement set, and PRECISE criteria apply with different emphases at each level.

Structure Indicators
What resources and systems exist?

P — Does the structural element enable patient-centred care?

R — Can staffing/equipment levels be reliably counted?

E — Is there evidence that the structure improves care?

C — Is the definition clear (e.g., consultant-delivered ward round within 14 hours)?

I — Is there variation in structure across AMUs?

S — Will structural change be detectable quickly?

E — Is structural data routinely collected (e.g., job planning, SAMBA staffing data)?

AMU examples: Consultant-of-the-week cover, AMU bed numbers, SDEC capacity, dedicated QI lead presence, 24/7 senior cover

Process Indicators
Are we doing the right things?

P — Is this process linked to something patients want?

R — Can the process be reliably observed or extracted from EPR?

E — Is there guideline evidence supporting this process (e.g., NICE, SAM standards)?

C — Is the operational definition unambiguous (e.g., "NEWS2 within 30 minutes of admission")?

I — Is there documented variation (SAMBA benchmarks)?

S — Will the process change show a signal across PDSA cycles within weeks?

E — Is process data in EPR audit trails or nursing charts?

AMU examples: NEWS2 documentation rate, Sepsis 6 bundle compliance, time to senior review, VTE assessment completion, SDEC conversion rate

Outcome Indicators
Did patients get better?

P — Is this outcome meaningful to patients (not just to clinicians)?

R — Is outcome data reliably coded (beware ICD-10 coding errors)?

E — Is there evidence this outcome reflects quality of care vs case mix?

C — Is the outcome definition standardised nationally?

I — Is there demonstrable variation between high and low performers?

S — Do outcomes move on a timescale detectable within the QI project?

E — Is outcome data routinely collected (SHMI, HES, PAS, mortality review)?

AMU examples: 30-day mortality, re-admission rate, length of stay, SHMI, patient satisfaction (Friends & Family Test, SAM-PLER)

Worked Example — Applying PRECISE to a Proposed AMU Indicator

PEFR Documentation in Acute Asthma
Criterion
Question Asked
Assessment for "PEFR within 30 min of presentation in acute asthma"
Rating
P — Patient-centred
Does this matter to patients?
PEFR guides acute management and risk-stratification — patients directly benefit from accurate assessment. BTS/SIGN guidelines endorse PEFR as patient-safety critical.
✓
R — Reliable
Consistent measurement?
PEFR value is objectively recorded by device. Documentation in EPR within 30 minutes is auditable. Risk: variability in who documents and in which field. Operational definition must specify EPR field.
~
E — Evidence-based
Evidence linking to quality?
BTS/SIGN British Guideline on the Management of Asthma (2022): PEFR is a mandatory component of acute asthma assessment. NCEPOD asthma reports have repeatedly identified failure to measure PEFR as a quality gap.
✓
C — Clear & Comparable
Unambiguous definition?
Defined as: proportion of adults admitted with acute asthma (ICD-10 J45) who have a PEFR value documented in EPR within 30 minutes of triage time. Exclusion: severe attack with immediate ITU transfer. Can be benchmarked via SAMBA respiratory sub-analysis.
✓
I — Improvable
Room for improvement?
Baseline audit at the AMU showed 41% compliance. National data from BTS asthma audit suggests median ~65%. Significant improvable gap identified. High clinical burden of acute asthma in this AMU provides adequate denominator.
✓
S — Sensitive to Change
Will it detect change?
Process measure close to the intervention (nursing training, clerking proforma change). High volume of acute asthma admissions (n~8–12/month) provides adequate power to detect change within 3 PDSA cycles (~6 weeks). Run chart viable.
✓
E — Evaluable
Feasible to collect?
Data extracted from EPR audit module. No additional data collection burden if EPR field is standardised. Monthly pull by the QI lead takes approximately 30 minutes. Sustainable beyond active project phase.
✓
✅ PRECISE Verdict: 6.5/7 — Highly suitable. This indicator is patient-centred, evidence-based, clearly defined, improvable, sensitive to change, and feasible to collect. The only partial is Reliability, mitigated by standardising the EPR field used for PEFR documentation — addressed as a PDSA change idea in its own right.
🔗 How PRECISE Relates to Other QI Frameworks

PRECISE is complementary to — not a replacement for — other QI frameworks used in Acute Medicine. Understanding how they fit together helps you use the right tool at the right stage of your project.

Model for Improvement (SAM-endorsed)

Provides the overarching improvement framework (three questions + PDSA). PRECISE is used within the third question: "What changes can we make?" — specifically when selecting which measures to use to track those changes.

SQUIRE 2.0

Guides the reporting of completed QI work. PRECISE supports SQUIRE Item 10 (Measures) — ensuring that the measures chosen meet the criteria for good indicator selection before the project begins.

Donabedian Triad

Provides the conceptual structure (Structure, Process, Outcome) for categorising indicators. PRECISE applies to all three types, helping to ensure that your selected indicator set covers all three Donabedian domains and each meets quality criteria.

SAMBA CQIs

SAMBA's Clinical Quality Indicators are pre-validated AMU process and outcome measures. They are designed to score well against PRECISE criteria — making them a robust starting point for AMU QI measurement plans.

NHS Good Indicators Guide

The definitive NHS guide to indicator selection (Pencheon, NHS Institute). PRECISE synthesises its criteria into a mnemonic adapted for Acute Medicine. Reading both provides comprehensive grounding in indicator selection methodology.

Portfolio Evidence — All Professions

Medical trainees use QIPAT at ARCP. Nurses align QI to KSF and NMC revalidation. ACPs use it for credentialing portfolios. Pharmacists map it to the Advanced Pharmacy Framework. AHPs use it for HCPC CPD records. PRECISE provides a documented rationale for indicator selection that strengthens portfolio evidence across all professional frameworks.

📚 Sources: PRECISE synthesises criteria from Pencheon D (NHS Good Indicators Guide, NHS Institute for Innovation & Improvement, 2007/2017); Campbell SM et al. (Qual Saf Health Care 2002;11:358–364); QUALIFY tool (Reiter A et al., 2008); and IOM/NQF quality indicator selection criteria.
